# Introduction

Patients have long turned to the internet to supplement—or challenge—medical advice, but the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots has fundamentally altered this landscape. Unlike static search engines, large language models (LLMs) engage users in conversational dialogue, offering personalized synthesis of complex medical information. Early evaluations have been promising: studies comparing AI responses to physician answers have found LLMs to be empathetic and generally accurate when answering patient questions about cancer. However, these assessments typically rely on a critical assumption: that patients ask questions neutrally, seeking objective truth.

In clinical reality, patients often approach medical information with preexisting beliefs. They may seek validation for unproven alternative therapies or confirmation of fears regarding conventional treatments. This distinction is crucial because LLMs are trained to be helpful and harmless, objectives that can conflict when a user's intent is misaligned with medical evidence. A growing body of computer science literature identifies "sycophancy"—the tendency of models to agree with users' stated views to maximize satisfaction—as a pervasive alignment failure. In general domains, this might result in an AI agreeing with a user's political preference; in oncology, it could mean validating a decision to forgo life-saving therapy.

The clinical stakes of such validation are severe. Patients with cancer who choose alternative medicine over conventional treatments face a twofold greater risk of death. If AI tools reinforce rather than correct misconceptions about these treatments, they may inadvertently accelerate pathways to treatment abandonment. Johnson et al. demonstrated that this risk is not theoretical; the decision to forgo conventional cancer therapy is often driven by misinformation encountered online or within social networks. As AI chatbots increasingly replace search engines as primary information sources, they inherit the responsibility to navigate these misconceptions safely.

However, current medical AI benchmarks primarily test performance on neutral, fact-seeking queries, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of how models perform under adversarial or bias-laden conditions. Prior evaluations, such as those by Pan et al. and Ayers et al., have established that models can provide accurate and empathetic responses when asked direct questions like "What are the common symptoms of lung cancer?" or "How does chemotherapy work?" While valuable, these studies test the model's knowledge retrieval capabilities under ideal conditions. They do not assess the model's robustness when a user asserts, "I heard chemotherapy is poison and I should take black salve instead." By omitting these adversarial scenarios, existing benchmarks may overestimate the safety of LLMs for real-world patient populations who often struggle with conflicting information.

This study sought to close that gap by stress-testing LLMs against the specific failure mode of sycophancy. We defined sycophancy in this context not merely as agreement, but as the failure to prioritize established medical evidence over user-provided misinformation. We designed a controlled experiment to determine whether LLMs maintain safety standards when users frame questions with misinformation—either by promoting unproven cures or attacking evidence-based therapies. By evaluating 14 leading models through a systematic factorial design, we aimed to quantify the "sycophancy gap" in medical AI safety—the difference in performance between neutral and biased queries—and identify factors, such as conversational depth, that might mitigate this risk. Our objective was to move beyond "can the model answer correctly?" to "will the model stand its ground?"
